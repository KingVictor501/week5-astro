Google Ads can put your business at the top of search results the moment someone looks for what you sell. That's powerful, but it's also easy to waste money if campaigns aren't set up carefully. This guide explains how Google Ads works for small businesses and how to build campaigns that bring in leads instead of just clicks.

<Callout title="TL;DR">
  Google Ads for small business works best when you start narrow: one campaign, tightly themed ad groups, high-intent keywords, negative keywords, a matching landing page, and conversion tracking from day one. Set a budget you can sustain for at least a couple of months, then review search terms weekly and move money toward what produces leads.
</Callout>

## How Google Ads works

Google Ads is an auction. When someone searches, Google decides which ads to show and in what order based on how much advertisers are willing to pay and how relevant and useful their ads and landing pages are. You usually pay only when someone clicks.

That second part matters. A business with a relevant ad and a helpful landing page can often outrank a competitor who simply bids more. Google summarizes relevance with a metric called **Quality Score**, which we cover in our guide to [improving your Google Ads Quality Score](/blog/improve-google-ads-quality-score).

## Choosing the right campaign type

Google offers several campaign types:

- **Search campaigns** show text ads on Google search results. For most small businesses looking for leads, this is the best place to start because you reach people actively searching.
- **Performance Max** uses Google's automation to show ads across Search, YouTube, Display, Gmail, and Maps. It can work well once you have solid conversion data. See whether [Performance Max is right for your small business](/blog/performance-max-for-small-business).
- **Display and video campaigns** build awareness but rarely produce leads as efficiently for a new advertiser.

Start with one Search campaign focused on your most profitable service.

## Structuring your account

A clean structure makes everything easier to manage:

1. **Campaign:** one service or one location, with its own budget.
2. **Ad groups:** small groups of closely related keywords, such as "water heater repair" and "water heater replacement."
3. **Ads:** responsive search ads written specifically for each ad group's keywords.

When the keyword, the ad, and the landing page all match, people are more likely to click and convert, and Google rewards that relevance.

## Picking keywords that bring customers

Focus on searches that signal someone is ready to hire or buy:

- Service plus location: "roof repair Springfield"
- Problem plus urgency: "emergency plumber"
- Comparison or hiring intent: "best accountant for small business"

Avoid broad, research-style keywords at first, like "how does a water heater work." They can be cheap per click but rarely turn into customers.

### Match types

Match types control how closely a search must match your keyword. Broad match reaches the most searches, phrase match requires the meaning of your phrase, and exact match is the tightest. Many small businesses start with phrase and exact match for control, then test broad match once conversion tracking is reliable.

## Negative keywords: your budget's best friend

Negative keywords stop your ads from showing for irrelevant searches, like job seekers or people looking for free tutorials. They're the fastest way to cut waste. Our guide to [negative keywords](/blog/negative-keywords-cut-wasted-google-ads-spend) includes a weekly routine for finding them.

## Writing ads that earn the click

Responsive search ads let you provide several headlines and descriptions, which Google combines automatically. Make them count:

- Include the main keyword in at least one headline.
- State a clear benefit or differentiator, such as same-day service or free estimates.
- Add a direct call to action: call now, book online, get a quote.
- Use assets (formerly extensions) for sitelinks, call buttons, and locations to take up more space.

## Sending clicks to the right page

Sending ad traffic to your homepage is one of the most common and expensive mistakes. Each ad group should point to a page that matches the search and makes the next step obvious. Our guide to [Google Ads landing pages](/blog/google-ads-landing-page-best-practices) covers what that page needs.

## Tracking what matters

Without conversion tracking, you can't tell which keywords produce leads, and Google's automated bidding has nothing to learn from. Track form submissions, phone calls, and bookings before you spend serious money. Google's overview of [conversion measurement](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722022) explains the options, and our step-by-step guide to [setting up Google Ads conversion tracking](/blog/google-ads-conversion-tracking-setup) walks through it.

## Setting a budget

Your budget should be large enough to collect meaningful data in your market. It depends on your cost per click, your conversion rate, and how many leads you need. Our guide to [setting a Google Ads budget for a small business](/blog/google-ads-budget-for-small-business) shows how to work it out from your own numbers.

## Reading your results: the metrics that matter

Google Ads reports dozens of metrics. For a small business focused on leads, a handful tell you almost everything:

| Metric | What it tells you | What to do with it |
|---|---|---|
| Conversions | How many leads your ads produced | Your main measure of success |
| Cost per conversion | What each lead costs | Compare it with what a customer is worth |
| Search impression share | How often your ads showed when they could have | Low share plus good results can justify more budget |
| Click-through rate | How appealing your ads are for the searches they show on | Low rates point to weak ads or irrelevant keywords |
| Search terms | The real searches that triggered your ads | Your best source of negatives and new keywords |

Impressions and clicks on their own can be misleading. A campaign with fewer clicks but more leads is the better campaign. Review these numbers over weeks, not days, so a single slow or busy day doesn't push you into the wrong decision.

## Google Ads and SEO work together

Google Ads and search engine optimization aren't competing choices. Ads deliver visibility as soon as campaigns launch, which is useful while organic rankings build. SEO builds visibility that keeps working without paying per click. Ads also reveal which searches turn into customers, which can guide what content you create for organic search. Many small businesses use ads for immediate leads and SEO for long-term growth, then rebalance as organic traffic grows.

## A weekly management routine

Google Ads isn't set-and-forget. Fifteen to thirty minutes a week keeps most small accounts healthy:

1. Review the search terms report and add negative keywords.
2. Pause keywords that spend without converting after a fair test.
3. Check that conversion tracking is still recording leads.
4. Compare cost per lead across ad groups and shift budget toward the best performers.
5. Test one new headline or description at a time.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Running ads without conversion tracking
- Using only broad match with no negative keywords
- Sending every click to the homepage
- Changing too many things at once, so you can't tell what worked
- Judging results after a few days instead of a few weeks
