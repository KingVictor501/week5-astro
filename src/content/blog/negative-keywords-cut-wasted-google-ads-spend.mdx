If you run Google Ads for your small business, some of your budget is almost certainly going to searches that will never turn into customers. Negative keywords are the simplest tool for stopping that, and most accounts don't use them nearly enough.

This guide explains what negative keywords do, how to find the ones your account needs, and a short weekly routine to keep wasted spend under control.

## What negative keywords actually do

When you add a keyword to a campaign, Google shows your ad for searches it considers related, and "related" is often broader than you'd expect. A negative keyword tells Google the opposite: never show my ad when a search contains this word or phrase.

Imagine a plumbing company bidding on "water heater repair." Without negatives, its ads can appear for searches like "water heater repair jobs," "water heater repair course," or "how to repair a water heater yourself." Each click costs the same as a click from a homeowner with a broken water heater, but almost none of them will ever book a service call.

<Callout title="The short version">
  Keywords decide where your ads can show. Negative keywords decide where they shouldn't. You need both.
</Callout>

## Where to find wasted searches

You don't need to guess. Google Ads keeps a record of the exact searches that triggered your ads.

### Start with the search terms report

In your Google Ads account, open a campaign and find the **Search terms** report (under Insights and reports). It lists the real searches people typed before clicking your ad, along with cost and conversions for each one.

Sort by cost, then work down the list and ask one question about each search: *Could this person realistically become a customer?* If the answer is no, it's a negative keyword candidate.

### Common categories of wasted searches

Most small business accounts end up blocking searches in a few familiar groups:

- **Job seekers:** jobs, careers, hiring, salary, internship
- **Do-it-yourself research:** how to, DIY, tutorial, guide
- **Free and cheap seekers:** free, template, cheap (if you don't compete on price)
- **Education:** course, class, certification, training
- **Wrong location:** cities or regions you don't serve
- **Competitor names:** unless you've decided to bid on them on purpose

Not every word on this list is wrong for every business. A training company *wants* the word "course." Treat the list as a starting point, not a rulebook.

## Choosing the right match type

Negative keywords have match types, and they behave a little differently from regular keywords:

- **Negative broad match** blocks searches that contain all of your words, in any order. Adding `free` blocks any search containing "free."
- **Negative phrase match** blocks searches that contain your words in that exact order. `"diy repair"` blocks "easy diy repair tips" but not "repair diy."
- **Negative exact match** blocks only that exact search. `[plumbing jobs]` blocks "plumbing jobs" and nothing else.

For single words that are always irrelevant, broad match is usually fine. For phrases, phrase match gives you more control and lowers the risk of blocking good traffic by accident.

## Use shared negative keyword lists

If you run more than one campaign, create a **negative keyword list** in the shared library and apply it to every relevant campaign. That way a negative you add once, like "jobs," protects your whole account, and you avoid the common problem of one campaign still paying for searches you blocked elsewhere.

<Callout type="note" title="Be careful with overlap">
  Before adding a negative, check it doesn't block searches you want. Blocking "repair" in a repair business would switch off most of your own ads.
</Callout>

## A 15-minute weekly routine

Negative keywords aren't a one-time task, because people keep searching in new ways. A short, regular review keeps waste from creeping back:

1. Open the search terms report for the last seven days.
2. Sort by cost, highest first.
3. Add obvious mismatches as negatives (phrase match for phrases, broad match for single words).
4. Add any good new searches as keywords, so you can bid on them on purpose.
5. Note anything you're unsure about and check it again next week.

Over a few weeks, the list of irrelevant searches gets shorter, and more of your budget goes to people who are actually looking to hire you.

## When to get help

If your search terms report is thousands of lines long, or you're not sure which searches are bringing in leads because conversion tracking isn't set up, it's worth getting a second set of eyes on the account. Clean tracking and a solid negative keyword list are two of the first things I check in any Google Ads audit.
