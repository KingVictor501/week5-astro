An accessible website can be used by everyone, including people who are blind or have low vision, people who are deaf or hard of hearing, and people who navigate with a keyboard instead of a mouse. For a small business, website accessibility means more potential customers, a clearer experience for all visitors, and less legal risk.

<Callout title="TL;DR">
  Follow WCAG as your guide. Start with the most common barriers: low color contrast, missing alt text, unlabeled form fields, keyboard traps, and missing focus outlines. Run quick checks yourself, fix the issues on your most important pages first, and build accessibility into every update.
</Callout>

## What website accessibility means

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, known as [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/), are the internationally recognized standard for accessible web content. They're organized around four principles. Content should be:

- **Perceivable:** people can see or hear it, for example through text alternatives for images
- **Operable:** people can navigate and use it, including with a keyboard
- **Understandable:** content and interactions are clear and predictable
- **Robust:** it works with assistive technologies like screen readers

WCAG has levels A, AA, and AAA. Level AA is the common target for business websites.

## Why it matters for small businesses

- **More customers.** Many people live with disabilities that affect how they use the web, and an inaccessible site turns them away.
- **Better experience for everyone.** Clear headings, readable text, and simple forms help all visitors, especially on phones.
- **Legal considerations.** In the United States, the Department of Justice has published [guidance on web accessibility and the ADA](https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/). Requirements vary by location and situation, so talk to a legal professional about your specific obligations.

## The most common accessibility barriers

### Low color contrast

Light gray text on a white background is hard to read for many people. Body text should have strong contrast against its background.

### Missing alternative text

Images that convey information need alt text describing them. Decorative images should have empty alt text so screen readers skip them.

### Unlabeled form fields

Every form field needs a visible label that's connected in the code. Placeholder text alone disappears when people type and isn't reliably announced.

### Keyboard problems

Some visitors can't use a mouse. They need to reach every link, button, and form field with the Tab key, and see where they are. Menus, pop-ups, and sliders are frequent trouble spots.

### Missing focus indicators

When someone tabs through your site, a visible outline should show which element is active. Some designs remove it for looks, leaving keyboard users lost.

### Poor heading structure

Headings help screen reader users scan a page. Use one H1, then H2s and H3s in a logical order, rather than choosing heading levels for their size.

## Quick checks you can run

You don't need special software to find many common problems. Start with these simple tests:

1. **Unplug your mouse** and try to navigate your homepage and contact form using only the keyboard.
2. **Zoom to 200%** and check that content still fits and remains usable.
3. **Check images** for meaningful alt text.
4. **Look at contrast**, especially light text and text over images.
5. **Review headings** to confirm they follow a logical outline.

Automated tools can catch some issues, but not all. Manual checks matter.

## Fix the most important pages first

Prioritize pages that matter most to customers: your homepage, service pages, and contact or booking page. Then work through the rest of the site.

## Build it into every update

Accessibility isn't a one-time project. Choose accessible themes and components, write alt text when adding images, label new forms properly, and check new pages with a keyboard before publishing.
