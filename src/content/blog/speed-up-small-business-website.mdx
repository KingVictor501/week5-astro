A slow website frustrates visitors, especially on phones, and many leave before your page finishes loading. Speed also affects how Google evaluates your pages. The good news is that most small business sites are slow for a handful of common, fixable reasons. Here's how to speed up a small business website, starting with the changes that make the biggest difference.

<Callout title="TL;DR">
  Test your key pages on mobile with PageSpeed Insights, then fix the big things first: oversized images, too many plugins and third-party scripts, missing caching, and slow hosting. Re-test after each change and check again every few months.
</Callout>

## Step 1: Measure before you change anything

Run your homepage and your most important service page through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/). Check the mobile results first, since that's how most visitors arrive.

Pay attention to:

- **Field data**, which reflects real visitors' experiences when your site has enough traffic
- **Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)**, how quickly the main content appears
- **The diagnostics section**, which points to specific causes

Write down your starting scores so you can measure progress.

## Step 2: Fix your images

Images are the most common cause of slow small business sites. A single photo straight from a phone can be many times larger than it needs to be.

- **Resize** images to the largest size they're actually displayed.
- **Compress** them and use modern formats like WebP or AVIF.
- **Lazy-load** images below the first screen, but not your main hero image.
- **Set width and height** so the page doesn't jump while loading.

## Step 3: Cut plugins and third-party scripts

Every plugin, widget, and tracking tag adds code the browser has to download and run. Over time, these pile up.

1. List every plugin and script on your site.
2. Remove anything you no longer use.
3. Replace heavy plugins with lighter alternatives or built-in features.
4. Delay non-essential scripts, like chat widgets, until after the page loads.

Common culprits include sliders, social media feeds, multiple analytics tools, and page builders loading features on every page.

## Step 4: Turn on caching

Caching stores ready-made copies of your pages so they don't have to be rebuilt for every visitor. Many hosts and platforms offer caching settings or plugins. Browser caching also lets returning visitors reuse files they've already downloaded.

## Step 5: Use a content delivery network

A content delivery network (CDN) stores copies of your files on servers around the world, so visitors download them from somewhere nearby. Many hosts include one, and it can noticeably speed up image-heavy sites.

## Step 6: Review your hosting

If your server is slow to respond, every page starts late, no matter how well it's optimized. Signs of a hosting problem include slow server response times in PageSpeed Insights and slowdowns during busy periods. Upgrading from very cheap shared hosting to a better plan or a performance-focused host is often worth it.

## Step 7: Simplify your fonts

Custom fonts add files to download. Limit the number of font families and weights, or use system fonts that load instantly. If you use web fonts, make sure text stays visible while they load.

## Step 8: Prioritize your main content

Help the browser show your most important content first. Google's guide to [optimizing Largest Contentful Paint](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-lcp) explains how to make sure the main image or headline isn't delayed by less important files.

## Step 9: Re-test and keep it fast

After each change, run PageSpeed Insights again to confirm it helped. Then make speed part of routine maintenance:

- Check key pages every quarter.
- Test speed before adding any new plugin or script.
- Compress every image before uploading.

## Know when to rebuild

Sometimes a site is slow because of its foundation: a heavy theme, an old page builder, or years of accumulated plugins. If fixes only help a little, a rebuild on a lighter platform can deliver a bigger leap than continued patching.
