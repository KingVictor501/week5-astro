Two small files on your website quietly shape how search engines explore it. Your XML sitemap and robots.txt file together tell Google which pages to find and which areas to skip. Getting them right takes minutes. Getting them wrong can hide your best pages from search.

<Callout title="TL;DR">
  Your XML sitemap should list every page you want in Google, and nothing else. Your robots.txt should block only areas that truly shouldn't be crawled, like admin pages, and point to your sitemap. Submit the sitemap in Google Search Console and recheck both after any redesign.
</Callout>

## What an XML sitemap does

An XML sitemap is a list of the URLs you want search engines to know about. Google's [sitemap overview](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/sitemaps/overview) explains that sitemaps help crawlers find pages more efficiently, especially on new sites or sites with pages that aren't well linked.

A sitemap doesn't force Google to index anything. It's a strong hint about what matters.

### What belongs in your sitemap

- Your homepage
- Service and product pages
- Location pages
- Blog posts and other content you want found
- Your about and contact pages

### What doesn't

- Pages marked `noindex`
- Duplicate URLs, such as tracking-parameter versions
- Redirected or deleted pages
- Thank-you pages and private landing pages

Most modern website platforms generate a sitemap automatically, often at `/sitemap.xml` or `/sitemap-index.xml`. Open yours in a browser and scan the list. If you see pages that shouldn't be there, or important pages are missing, fix that first.

If your sitemap is generated by a plugin, look for its settings. Most let you exclude specific page types, such as tag archives or author pages, which are rarely useful in search for a small business site.

## What robots.txt does

Robots.txt is a plain text file at the root of your site, such as `example.com/robots.txt`. It gives crawlers instructions about which paths they may visit. Google's [robots.txt introduction](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots/intro) covers the details.

A sensible robots.txt for most small business sites looks like this:

```txt
User-agent: *
Allow: /
Disallow: /admin/

Sitemap: https://www.example.com/sitemap-index.xml
```

## Common mistakes to avoid

### Blocking the whole site

`Disallow: /` under `User-agent: *` tells every crawler to stay away from every page. It's common on sites that were blocked during development and never unblocked. Check for it first.

### Using robots.txt to hide pages from search

Robots.txt controls crawling, not indexing. A blocked page can still appear in results if other sites link to it. To keep a page out of Google, use a `noindex` tag and let Google crawl the page so it can see that tag.

### Blocking CSS or JavaScript

Google renders pages much like a browser. Blocking the files that style or power your pages can make them look broken to Google.

### Leaving old URLs in the sitemap

After a redesign, sitemaps sometimes keep listing old URLs that now redirect or return errors. That wastes crawl attention and sends mixed signals.

## Submit and monitor in Search Console

In Google Search Console, open **Sitemaps** and submit your sitemap URL. Search Console reports whether it was read successfully and how many URLs it found. Check back after any major change.

## A five-minute check

1. Visit `yourdomain.com/robots.txt` and make sure nothing important is disallowed.
2. Confirm the file lists your sitemap.
3. Open your sitemap and scan for missing or unwanted pages.
4. Check the Sitemaps report in Search Console for errors.
5. Repeat after every redesign or platform change.
